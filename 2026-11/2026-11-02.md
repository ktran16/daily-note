## 00:04 - [Data Engineering] Flink Idle Sources: The Silent Kafka Partition That Freezes Your Watermark

Your event-time windowed job works perfectly in dev against a replayed topic. In production it runs green, consumes records, checkpoints fine — and never emits a single window. Backpressure is zero, state size climbs forever. The culprit is almost always one quiet Kafka partition.

**The mechanism.** A watermark is not global. Every source subtask generates its own watermark from the records it reads, and every downstream operator computes its watermark as the **minimum across all its input channels**. That minimum is what makes the semantics sound: an operator can only claim "no events before T" once *every* upstream path has said so. But it also means a single input stuck at `Long.MIN_VALUE` pins the whole job at `Long.MIN_VALUE`. A partition that receives no traffic never produces a record, so it never advances a watermark, so no window anywhere in the graph ever fires.

This bites when a topic is over-partitioned relative to traffic, when the producer's partition key leaves some partitions dormant (regional or tenant keys at 3 a.m.), or during low-volume off-hours.

**The fix** is to let a quiet channel step out of the minimum:

```java
WatermarkStrategy
    .<Order>forBoundedOutOfOrderness(Duration.ofSeconds(30))
    .withTimestampAssigner((e, ts) -> e.eventTimeMillis())
    .withIdleness(Duration.ofMinutes(1));
```

In Flink SQL, the equivalent is a config setting, not DDL — the `WATERMARK FOR` clause has no idleness option:

```sql
SET 'table.exec.source.idle-timeout' = '1 min';
```

After the timeout, the channel is marked idle and excluded from the min until it produces again.

**The caveat that matters.** Idleness is a *liveness* fix, not a correctness one. Once you exclude a channel, the watermark advances without it — so when that partition wakes up, its records are genuinely late and will be dropped unless you catch them (`allowedLateness` plus `sideOutputLateData`). Set the timeout comfortably longer than your normal quiet gaps, or you trade a stalled job for silent data loss.

**Diagnosing it:** watch the per-subtask `currentOutputWatermark` metric. If one subtask reads `-9223372036854775808` while the others advance, you've found your partition.

## 05:03 - [Data Analytics] Cluster-Randomized Tests: The 20-User Store That Counts as One Observation

You randomize at the store, team, or city level — because treatment spills over between users at the same location, or because the feature is operational and can't be split per person. Then you analyze it the way you always do: one row per user, a two-sample t-test, `p = 0.02`, ship it. That p-value is fiction.

**The mechanism.** A t-test assumes independent observations. Users inside one cluster share a manager, a menu, a local promotion, weather — so their outcomes correlate. The size of that correlation is the **intraclass correlation (ICC)**, ρ. Treating correlated users as independent understates the variance by the *design effect*:

```
VIF = 1 + (m - 1) × ρ
```

where `m` is the average users per cluster. With 40 stores × 20 users = 800 users and a modest ρ = 0.10:

```
VIF = 1 + 19 × 0.10 = 2.9
```

Your effective sample size is 800 / 2.9 ≈ **276 users**, and your standard error is √2.9 = **1.7x wider** than reported. The nominal 5% false-positive rate lands closer to 20%. Note what dominates: at ρ = 0.10, growing a cluster from 20 to 200 users raises VIF from 2.9 to 20.9 — more users per cluster buys almost nothing. **Power comes from more clusters, not bigger ones.**

**The fix** that needs no new tooling: collapse to the randomization unit first, then test cluster means.

```sql
-- one row per store, not per user
SELECT variant, store_id,
       AVG(converted::int) AS store_rate,
       COUNT(*)            AS n_users
FROM   exposures
GROUP  BY variant, store_id;
-- then: t-test on store_rate, n = 40, df = 38
```

Estimate ρ before launch from historical data, using a one-way ANOVA on a pre-period:

```python
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf
m = smf.mixedlm("converted ~ 1", pre, groups=pre["store_id"]).fit()
icc = m.cov_re.iloc[0, 0] / (m.cov_re.iloc[0, 0] + m.scale)
```

**Practical rule:** fewer than ~30 clusters per arm and no cluster-level test will save you — the degrees of freedom are gone. Redesign (switchback over time within each store) rather than reinterpret.

## 10:03 - [ML & AI] Quantile Regression: Your P90 Model *Should* Look Worse on RMSE

Squared error asks for the conditional mean. Plenty of problems don't want it. Inventory wants "stock enough to cover 90% of days." Capacity planning wants a p99 latency. Delivery estimates want a window, not a point. For those, train the quantile directly with **pinball loss**:

```
L_τ(y, ŷ) = max( τ·(y − ŷ),  (τ−1)·(y − ŷ) )
          = τ·(y − ŷ)      if y ≥ ŷ   (under-predicted)
            (1−τ)·(ŷ − y)  if y <  ŷ   (over-predicted)
```

For τ = 0.9 an under-prediction costs **9×** an over-prediction, so the minimizer climbs until only 10% of actuals sit above it. That's the whole mechanism — an asymmetric penalty whose balance point *is* the quantile.

### The reversal, numerically

Ten observed demands: `[10, 12, 15, 18, 20, 22, 25, 30, 40, 90]`.

| prediction | pinball(τ=0.9) | RMSE | actuals ≤ pred |
|---|---|---|---|
| 28.2 (the mean) | 7.54 | **22.25** | 70% |
| 40 (the p90) | **6.18** | 25.18 | **90%** |

Switching to the quantile model makes RMSE **13% worse** and that is the model working correctly. The mean minimizes RMSE by definition; nothing can beat it there. If you train a p90 model and grade it on RMSE, you will always "discover" it's worse and revert. Grade it on pinball loss plus **empirical coverage** — what fraction of held-out actuals fall below the prediction. You want 90%; 70% means you're under-serving, 99% means you're paying for inventory you don't need.

### Three things that bite

**Quantile crossing.** One model per τ means nothing couples them, so individual rows come back with p10 > p90. Sort the predictions row-wise as a post-process, or use a multi-quantile objective.

**Zero Hessian.** The pinball gradient is the constant ±τ, so the second derivative is 0 and the usual Newton leaf value is undefined. LightGBM handles this by renewing each leaf's output as a weighted percentile of its residuals. Practical consequence: Hessian-based regularization (`min_child_weight`) loses its meaning here — control leaf size with `min_data_in_leaf` instead.

**Your loss must match the decision.** If stockouts cost 4× overstock, the optimal service level is 4/(4+1) = 0.8. Derive τ from the cost ratio; don't default to 0.95 because it sounds safe.

```python
import lightgbm as lgb, numpy as np

models = {}
for tau in (0.1, 0.5, 0.9):
    models[tau] = lgb.LGBMRegressor(
        objective="quantile", alpha=tau,   # alpha IS tau in LightGBM
        n_estimators=500, learning_rate=0.05,
        min_data_in_leaf=50,               # not min_child_weight
    ).fit(X_tr, y_tr)

preds = np.sort(                            # kills quantile crossing
    np.column_stack([models[t].predict(X_te) for t in (0.1, 0.5, 0.9)]),
    axis=1,
)
lo, mid, hi = preds.T

for name, p, tau in [("p10", lo, 0.1), ("p90", hi, 0.9)]:
    d = y_te - p
    print(f"{name}: coverage={np.mean(y_te <= p):.3f} (target {tau:.2f})  "
          f"pinball={np.mean(np.maximum(tau*d, (tau-1)*d)):.3f}")
```

Sklearn equivalents: `GradientBoostingRegressor(loss="quantile", alpha=0.9)`, or `QuantileRegressor` for the linear case. Note this gives you *conditional* quantiles — coverage holds on average across the test set, not per-row. If you need a per-instance guarantee without trusting the model, that's conformal prediction's job, and the two compose well: quantile regression for the interval shape, conformalization to correct its width.

## 16:12 - [Crypto & Business] UTXOs vs Account Balances: Why Your Bitcoin Fee Depends on Your Deposit History

Ethereum uses an **account model**: your balance is one number in a global state table. Bitcoin uses the **UTXO model**: your "balance" is just the sum of discrete unspent outputs, each a separate coin you must spend whole. That difference means two wallets holding the same 0.1 BTC can pay wildly different fees to send it.

**Fees are priced per vbyte, not per bitcoin.** Each input you consume adds bytes. For native SegWit (P2WPKH), roughly:

| Component | vbytes |
|---|---|
| Overhead | ~10.5 |
| Each input | ~68 |
| Each output | ~31 |

Sending to one address with change, at 20 sat/vB:

- **Wallet A** — one 0.1 BTC UTXO: `10.5 + 68 + 62 = 140.5 vB` → **2,810 sats** (~$2.80 at $100k/BTC)
- **Wallet B** — twenty 0.005 BTC UTXOs from faucet-sized deposits: `10.5 + 1,360 + 62 = 1,432.5 vB` → **28,650 sats** (~$28.65)

Same amount sent, **10× the fee**, purely because of how the coins arrived. Taproot inputs (~57.5 vB) are cheaper; legacy P2PKH (~148 vB) is far worse.

**Dust is an economic concept, not just a rule.** Bitcoin Core relays a P2WPKH output as dust below 294 sats. But the practical threshold is higher: at 50 sat/vB, spending one input costs 68 × 50 = 3,400 sats. Any UTXO below that is unspendable in practice — it costs more to move than it's worth. Exchanges' tiny "test" withdrawals create exactly this.

**The practical move: consolidate when fees are low.** Spending 20 inputs into 1 output is `10.5 + 1,360 + 31 = 1,401.5 vB`. At 2 sat/vB that's 2,803 sats; the identical transaction at 60 sat/vB costs 84,090 sats — a 30× difference for waiting. The cost is privacy: consolidating proves all those addresses share one owner, permanently linking them on-chain.

Check your wallet's coin-control view. If you see dozens of small UTXOs, you're carrying a fee liability that only grows with the next fee spike.
