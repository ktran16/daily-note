## 00:02 - [Data Engineering] Snowflake Time Travel and Fail-safe: The Storage Bill Nobody Queries

Snowflake storage is not just your data. Every permanent table bills three buckets: **active bytes** (the current version), **Time Travel bytes** (old versions kept for `DATA_RETENTION_TIME_IN_DAYS`), and **Fail-safe bytes** (a further **7 days**, not configurable, recoverable only by Snowflake support).

That means a permanent table rebuilt daily with `CREATE OR REPLACE` keeps roughly `1 + retention + 7` generations of churn on the bill. A 1 TB table fully refreshed every night can quietly cost 8x its size, and nothing in the query history tells you.

Find it with `ACCOUNT_USAGE`:

```sql
select table_catalog, table_schema, table_name,
       round(active_bytes      / pow(1024,4), 2) as active_tb,
       round(time_travel_bytes / pow(1024,4), 2) as time_travel_tb,
       round(failsafe_bytes    / pow(1024,4), 2) as failsafe_tb
from snowflake.account_usage.table_storage_metrics
where not deleted
order by failsafe_bytes desc
limit 20;
```

If `failsafe_tb` dwarfs `active_tb`, the table is rewriting itself wholesale.

**The fix is table type, not retention.** Transient tables have no Fail-safe at all and cap Time Travel at 1 day. dbt-snowflake already creates models as transient by default — the leaks are the things that opt out or were never dbt tables: snapshots (always permanent, which is correct), hand-rolled DDL, and anyone who set `transient: false` to get recovery they never use.

```yaml
models:
  my_project:
    staging:
      +transient: true          # rebuildable from source — no Fail-safe needed
    marts:
      +transient: false         # keep recoverable
      +post-hook: "alter table {{ this }} set data_retention_time_in_days = 7"
```

Two gotchas:

- **Lowering retention is destructive and immediate.** Dropping a table from 90 to 1 day starts purging older versions right away; you cannot get them back by raising it again.
- **Dropping a permanent table does not stop the meter.** Its Time Travel and Fail-safe bytes keep billing through the full window. Converting a table to transient requires a recreate, so do it at the next scheduled full refresh rather than as an emergency cost fix.

Decide recoverability per layer deliberately. Paying Fail-safe on a staging table you can rebuild in four minutes is pure waste.

## 05:02 - [Data Analytics] Mix-Shift Decomposition: The Residual You Create by Mixing Base Periods

ARPU dropped from **$50.00 to $45.35** last quarter. Every segment's ARPU *rose*. Leadership wants to know which it is.

| Segment | Users (prior) | ARPU | Users (now) | ARPU |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Enterprise | 2,000 | $150 | 1,800 | $155 |
| SMB | 8,000 | $25 | 10,200 | $26 |
| **Total** | **10,000** | **$50.00** | **12,000** | **$45.35** |

Nothing degraded. SMB grew 27% while Enterprise shrank 10%, so the high-ARPU segment's *weight* fell from 20% to 15%. The aggregate ARPU change is a weighted-average artifact, and shift-share decomposition splits it into **rate** (within-segment change) and **mix** (reweighting).

### The three-term form

With `w` as user share and `r` as segment ARPU:

- **Rate** = Σ w₀(r₁−r₀) = 0.20(+5) + 0.80(+1) = **+1.80**
- **Mix** = Σ (w₁−w₀)r₀ = (−0.05)(150) + (+0.05)(25) = **−6.25**
- **Interaction** = Σ (w₁−w₀)(r₁−r₀) = **−0.20**

Sum: −4.65. Exact. Mix is 134% of the move; the "ARPU is falling" narrative is really "the mix is shifting to SMB."

### The trap

Three combinations are algebraically exact, because Σw₁r₁ − Σw₀r₀ telescopes:

- w₀-weighted rate + r₀-weighted mix + interaction
- **w₁**-weighted rate + r₀-weighted mix (two terms, no residual)
- w₀-weighted rate + **r₁**-weighted mix (two terms, no residual)

One is wrong: w₁-weighted rate + r₁-weighted mix double-counts the interaction and returns **−4.85** against an actual **−4.65**. People hit this by computing each effect in isolation with "current" numbers. The tell is components that don't reconcile to the headline delta — always assert that they do.

```sql
WITH d AS (
  SELECT segment,
         u0 * 1.0 / SUM(u0) OVER () AS w0,
         u1 * 1.0 / SUM(u1) OVER () AS w1,
         rev0 / NULLIF(u0, 0)       AS r0,
         rev1 / NULLIF(u1, 0)       AS r1
  FROM segment_periods
)
SELECT segment,
       (w1 - w0) * r0        AS mix_effect,
       w0 * (r1 - r0)        AS rate_effect,
       (w1 - w0) * (r1 - r0) AS interaction
FROM d
ORDER BY ABS((w1 - w0) * r0) DESC;
```

Cast to float explicitly — integer division silently makes every share 0. And state your base period in the chart footnote: the mix/rate split is not unique, and two analysts using different conventions will disagree by the interaction term.

## 10:03 - [ML & AI] Uplift Modeling: Your Churn Model Ranks the Wrong Customers

A churn model predicts `P(churn | x)`. A retention campaign needs something else: `P(stay | x, treated) − P(stay | x, untreated)`. Those two rankings are not the same, and optimizing the first wastes most of the budget.

Every customer sits in one of four cells:

| | Stays if treated | Churns if treated |
|---|---|---|
| **Stays if not treated** | Sure thing (wasted spend) | **Sleeping dog** (harm) |
| **Churns if not treated** | **Persuadable** (the target) | Lost cause (wasted spend) |

A churn score ranks lost causes and persuadables together at the top, because both churn without treatment. Only one of them responds.

**Worked example.** 100K customers, 50% randomly offered a 20% discount, bucketed by churn-model decile:

| Decile | Retention (control) | Retention (treated) | Uplift |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 (highest churn risk) | 20% | 23% | +3 pp |
| 5 | 70% | 79% | **+9 pp** |
| 10 (lowest risk) | 92% | 89% | **−3 pp** |

Decile 1 is mostly lost causes. Decile 5 holds the persuadables. Decile 10 is sleeping dogs — the email reminded a dormant subscriber they were paying. Mailing by churn rank buys 3 pp where 9 pp was available, and actively loses money at the bottom.

**Fitting it (T-learner).**

```python
from sklearn.ensemble import HistGradientBoostingClassifier as GBC

m1 = GBC().fit(X[t == 1], y[t == 1])   # treated arm
m0 = GBC().fit(X[t == 0], y[t == 0])   # control arm
uplift = m1.predict_proba(Xn)[:, 1] - m0.predict_proba(Xn)[:, 1]
```

Watch the variance: you are subtracting two independently-fit estimates, each carrying maybe ±2 pp of noise, to recover a 3–9 pp signal. Errors add in quadrature, so the difference is noisier than either model. An S-learner (one model, treatment as a feature plus explicit interactions) or an X-learner is usually steadier at small effect sizes.

**Two non-negotiables.** Treatment assignment must be randomized — otherwise you are modeling who marketing chose, not what the offer did. And you cannot score this with AUC, because no individual has both outcomes. Use a Qini curve: cumulative incremental conversions versus number targeted, against the random-targeting diagonal. The peak of that curve is your campaign size.
