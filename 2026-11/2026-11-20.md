## 00:03 - [Data Engineering] Snowflake Resource Monitors: The Guardrail That Ignores Serverless Spend

A resource monitor is the only hard stop Snowflake gives you on credit burn: it tracks virtual warehouse credits against a quota and fires triggers when thresholds are crossed. Every team sets one up after their first surprise bill, and most set it up wrong.

```sql
USE ROLE ACCOUNTADMIN;

CREATE OR REPLACE RESOURCE MONITOR rm_transform
  WITH CREDIT_QUOTA   = 500
       FREQUENCY      = MONTHLY
       START_TIMESTAMP = IMMEDIATELY
  TRIGGERS ON 75  PERCENT DO NOTIFY
           ON 100 PERCENT DO SUSPEND
           ON 110 PERCENT DO SUSPEND_IMMEDIATE;

ALTER WAREHOUSE wh_dbt_prod SET RESOURCE MONITOR = rm_transform;
```

**Three things that surprise people.**

*It does not cover serverless.* The quota counts warehouse compute only. Snowpipe, automatic clustering, materialized view and dynamic table refreshes, search optimization, replication and Cortex functions are serverless — they bill outside any resource monitor, account-level one included. If your spike came from auto-clustering a badly keyed table, the monitor sat at 20% while the bill tripled. Those need `ALTER ACCOUNT SET ... BUDGET` (Budgets) or a watch on `SERVERLESS_TASK_HISTORY` / `AUTOMATIC_CLUSTERING_HISTORY`.

*`SUSPEND` and `SUSPEND_IMMEDIATE` are different promises.* `SUSPEND` lets in-flight statements finish, then blocks new ones. `SUSPEND_IMMEDIATE` cancels running queries mid-flight. Put the gentle one at 100% and the brutal one at 110% so a single long-running `dbt build` isn't killed by a threshold it crossed in its last minute.

*Enforcement lags.* Credit accounting is periodic, not per-second, so overshoot past the quota is normal. Treat the quota as a tripwire, not a cap.

Two more sharp edges: a warehouse belongs to exactly **one** monitor, and the account-level monitor applies on top — whichever trips first wins. And a `NOTIFY` trigger emails nothing unless recipients are configured; in current Snowflake, point it at a notification integration rather than relying on admins having toggled email on.

```sql
SELECT name, credit_quota, used_credits, remaining_credits
FROM SNOWFLAKE.ACCOUNT_USAGE.RESOURCE_MONITORS;
```

Pattern that works: a generous account-level monitor as the backstop, per-warehouse monitors sized to each team, and Budgets for everything serverless.

## 05:03 - [Data Analytics] Correlation on Aggregated Data: r = 0.88 Between Countries, 0.01 Within Them

A deck lands on your desk: "Marketing spend and conversion rate, r = 0.88 across 40 countries — spend drives conversion." The chart is 40 neat dots hugging a line. The same relationship measured at the user level is r = 0.07, and once you remove country differences it is 0.01. Nothing drives anything.

This is aggregation bias (the ecological fallacy). Correlation is covariance over the product of standard deviations. Averaging within a group collapses the within-group noise in that denominator while leaving the between-group signal intact, so `r` inflates mechanically — no new relationship required.

```python
import numpy as np, pandas as pd
rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
n_country, n_user = 40, 500
g = np.repeat(np.arange(n_country), n_user)

country_spend = rng.normal(0, 1, n_country)
country_conv  = 0.9 * country_spend + rng.normal(0, 0.4, n_country)

df = pd.DataFrame({
    "country": g,
    # within-country noise (sd 3) swamps the country-level spread (sd 1)
    "spend": country_spend[g] + rng.normal(0, 3, n_country * n_user),
    "conv":  country_conv[g]  + rng.normal(0, 3, n_country * n_user),
})

print(df["spend"].corr(df["conv"]))                       # 0.07  (n = 20,000)
agg = df.groupby("country")[["spend", "conv"]].mean()
print(agg["spend"].corr(agg["conv"]))                     # 0.88  (n = 40)

# within-country: subtract each country's mean from both columns
w = df.assign(s=df["spend"] - df.groupby("country")["spend"].transform("mean"),
              c=df["conv"]  - df.groupby("country")["conv"].transform("mean"))
print(w["s"].corr(w["c"]))                                # 0.01
```

The user-level data has *no* individual relationship by construction, yet the aggregate reads as a near-textbook linear fit.

It cuts the other way too: aggregation can bury or flip a real within-group effect, which is why a GROUP BY in front of a `CORR()` is never a neutral step.

Practical rules:

- **Correlate at the grain of the decision.** If you will act per user, measure per user.
- **Report n honestly.** 40 countries is n = 40, not 20,000. Significance stars computed on the row count of the pre-aggregation table are fiction.
- **Decompose rather than choose.** Report between-group and within-group correlation side by side; a large gap *is* the finding.
- **Be suspicious of tidy scatterplots** where each dot is a mean of thousands. Tightness there is often evidence of averaging, not of a mechanism.

## 10:04 - [ML & AI] Gradient Clipping: The `unscale_` You Forgot Divides Your Step by 65,536

Gradient clipping is the one line everyone copies into a training loop and nobody logs. It has two forms that do different things, and one ordering bug with mixed precision that silently near-zeroes your updates.

**Norm vs value.** `clip_grad_norm_` treats every parameter's gradient as one concatenated vector, measures its L2 norm, and rescales *everything* by `max_norm / total_norm` if that exceeds 1. Direction is preserved; only magnitude is capped. Two tensors with gradient norms 3.0 and 4.0 give a total norm of √(9+16) = 5.0; with `max_norm=1.0` both are multiplied by 0.2, landing at 0.6 and 0.8, total exactly 1.0.

`clip_grad_value_` instead clamps each *element* to ±c independently. That changes the gradient's direction — a coordinate at 50 and one at 0.01 both survive as 0.1 and 0.01. Use norm clipping unless you specifically want per-element saturation.

**The AMP bug.** Under `GradScaler`, `backward()` leaves gradients multiplied by the scale factor S (often 65536). Clip before unscaling and you compute a norm S× too big, so clipping forces the *scaled* gradient to norm `max_norm` — and `scaler.step` then divides by S. Your effective update norm is `max_norm / S` = 1.0/65536 ≈ **1.5e-5**. Not a no-op: a 65,000× smaller step that jumps around every time the scaler adapts S. Loss curves go flat and the config looks fine.

```python
scaler.scale(loss).backward()
scaler.unscale_(optimizer)                                   # <- required first
norm = torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), 1.0)
scaler.step(optimizer); scaler.update(); optimizer.zero_grad(set_to_none=True)
```

`clip_grad_norm_` returns the **pre-clip** norm — log that, it is the diagnostic.

**Two more.** With gradient accumulation, clip once after the final micro-batch backward; clipping each micro-batch bounds K separate norms and nothing about their sum. And don't default `max_norm=1.0` blindly: log norms for 200 steps first. If typical norm is 0.3, clipping only catches spikes (correct). If it is 12, you clip every step and have quietly switched to normalized gradient descent, where your LR schedule is the only thing setting step size.

## 15:03 - [Crypto & Business] Proof of Reserves: What a Merkle Attestation Proves, and the Three Things It Doesn't

A "Proof of Reserves" page is an exchange's answer to *are my coins there?* It does real cryptographic work — and then stops well short of where most readers assume it ends.

**The mechanism.** The exchange builds a **Merkle sum tree** of customer liabilities. Each leaf is one account: `hash(user_id_salt, balance)` plus the balance itself. Every parent node stores the hash of its children *and the sum of their balances*. The root therefore commits to one number — total customer BTC, say 412,805 BTC — and to every individual balance underneath it.

You verify your slice with a **Merkle proof**: the exchange gives you your leaf plus the ~20 sibling hashes and sibling sums on the path to the root (log₂ of 1M accounts ≈ 20 hops). You recompute upward. If your final hash and sum match the published root, two things are true: your balance was included, and it was included at full value. An exchange that quietly halves your balance to shrink its liabilities breaks the sums; one that omits you breaks nothing *for other users* — which is exactly why you must check your own proof.

The asset side is separate: signed messages or on-chain transfers from wallets holding ≥ the root's total.

**The three gaps.**

1. **No proof of liabilities-completeness.** The root commits to the accounts included. Omitted accounts, or off-book obligations — a loan to an affiliate, a bond sold to a VC — are invisible. Reserves can be 101% of *disclosed* liabilities and 60% of real ones. This is the FTX-shaped hole.
2. **No proof of ownership.** A signature proves someone with the key signed. Borrowed coins, or coins shared across two exchanges attesting on different days, sign just as well. Mitigation: simultaneous attestation and proof of *exclusive* control — rarely provided.
3. **Point-in-time only.** It is a snapshot, typically monthly or quarterly. Solvent at 00:00 UTC on the 1st says nothing about the 14th.

**Practical read.** Treat a PoR as a floor, not a clearance: verify *your* leaf, check attestation frequency and whether an auditor signed the liability side, and note that none of it makes an exchange balance a custody arrangement you control. Self-custody changes the question from "do I trust this attestation" to "do I control this key."
