## 00:03 - [Data Engineering] Snowflake Resource Monitors: The Guardrail That Ignores Serverless Spend

A resource monitor is the only hard stop Snowflake gives you on credit burn: it tracks virtual warehouse credits against a quota and fires triggers when thresholds are crossed. Every team sets one up after their first surprise bill, and most set it up wrong.

```sql
USE ROLE ACCOUNTADMIN;

CREATE OR REPLACE RESOURCE MONITOR rm_transform
  WITH CREDIT_QUOTA   = 500
       FREQUENCY      = MONTHLY
       START_TIMESTAMP = IMMEDIATELY
  TRIGGERS ON 75  PERCENT DO NOTIFY
           ON 100 PERCENT DO SUSPEND
           ON 110 PERCENT DO SUSPEND_IMMEDIATE;

ALTER WAREHOUSE wh_dbt_prod SET RESOURCE MONITOR = rm_transform;
```

**Three things that surprise people.**

*It does not cover serverless.* The quota counts warehouse compute only. Snowpipe, automatic clustering, materialized view and dynamic table refreshes, search optimization, replication and Cortex functions are serverless — they bill outside any resource monitor, account-level one included. If your spike came from auto-clustering a badly keyed table, the monitor sat at 20% while the bill tripled. Those need `ALTER ACCOUNT SET ... BUDGET` (Budgets) or a watch on `SERVERLESS_TASK_HISTORY` / `AUTOMATIC_CLUSTERING_HISTORY`.

*`SUSPEND` and `SUSPEND_IMMEDIATE` are different promises.* `SUSPEND` lets in-flight statements finish, then blocks new ones. `SUSPEND_IMMEDIATE` cancels running queries mid-flight. Put the gentle one at 100% and the brutal one at 110% so a single long-running `dbt build` isn't killed by a threshold it crossed in its last minute.

*Enforcement lags.* Credit accounting is periodic, not per-second, so overshoot past the quota is normal. Treat the quota as a tripwire, not a cap.

Two more sharp edges: a warehouse belongs to exactly **one** monitor, and the account-level monitor applies on top — whichever trips first wins. And a `NOTIFY` trigger emails nothing unless recipients are configured; in current Snowflake, point it at a notification integration rather than relying on admins having toggled email on.

```sql
SELECT name, credit_quota, used_credits, remaining_credits
FROM SNOWFLAKE.ACCOUNT_USAGE.RESOURCE_MONITORS;
```

Pattern that works: a generous account-level monitor as the backstop, per-warehouse monitors sized to each team, and Budgets for everything serverless.

## 05:03 - [Data Analytics] Correlation on Aggregated Data: r = 0.88 Between Countries, 0.01 Within Them

A deck lands on your desk: "Marketing spend and conversion rate, r = 0.88 across 40 countries — spend drives conversion." The chart is 40 neat dots hugging a line. The same relationship measured at the user level is r = 0.07, and once you remove country differences it is 0.01. Nothing drives anything.

This is aggregation bias (the ecological fallacy). Correlation is covariance over the product of standard deviations. Averaging within a group collapses the within-group noise in that denominator while leaving the between-group signal intact, so `r` inflates mechanically — no new relationship required.

```python
import numpy as np, pandas as pd
rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
n_country, n_user = 40, 500
g = np.repeat(np.arange(n_country), n_user)

country_spend = rng.normal(0, 1, n_country)
country_conv  = 0.9 * country_spend + rng.normal(0, 0.4, n_country)

df = pd.DataFrame({
    "country": g,
    # within-country noise (sd 3) swamps the country-level spread (sd 1)
    "spend": country_spend[g] + rng.normal(0, 3, n_country * n_user),
    "conv":  country_conv[g]  + rng.normal(0, 3, n_country * n_user),
})

print(df["spend"].corr(df["conv"]))                       # 0.07  (n = 20,000)
agg = df.groupby("country")[["spend", "conv"]].mean()
print(agg["spend"].corr(agg["conv"]))                     # 0.88  (n = 40)

# within-country: subtract each country's mean from both columns
w = df.assign(s=df["spend"] - df.groupby("country")["spend"].transform("mean"),
              c=df["conv"]  - df.groupby("country")["conv"].transform("mean"))
print(w["s"].corr(w["c"]))                                # 0.01
```

The user-level data has *no* individual relationship by construction, yet the aggregate reads as a near-textbook linear fit.

It cuts the other way too: aggregation can bury or flip a real within-group effect, which is why a GROUP BY in front of a `CORR()` is never a neutral step.

Practical rules:

- **Correlate at the grain of the decision.** If you will act per user, measure per user.
- **Report n honestly.** 40 countries is n = 40, not 20,000. Significance stars computed on the row count of the pre-aggregation table are fiction.
- **Decompose rather than choose.** Report between-group and within-group correlation side by side; a large gap *is* the finding.
- **Be suspicious of tidy scatterplots** where each dot is a mean of thousands. Tightness there is often evidence of averaging, not of a mechanism.
