## 00:04 - [Data Engineering] Write-Audit-Publish: Testing Data Before Anyone Can Read It

The default pipeline shape is write-then-test: load the table, then run your quality checks. If a check fails, you alert — but the bad data has already been queryable for however long the tests took. On a wide mart with twenty tests, that is a five-minute window where every dashboard, reverse-ETL sync, and downstream model reads rows you are about to declare invalid.

Write-Audit-Publish (WAP) closes the window by making publication a separate, atomic step. You write to a location nobody reads, audit *that* version, and only then swap it into place.

**Iceberg** implements this natively with branches:

```sql
ALTER TABLE prod.orders SET TBLPROPERTIES ('write.wap.enabled'='true');
```

```python
spark.conf.set("spark.wap.branch", "etl_20260921")   # writes land on the branch
spark.sql("INSERT INTO prod.orders SELECT * FROM staged")
# audits read the branch, not main
bad = spark.sql("SELECT count(*) FROM prod.orders WHERE order_total < 0").first()[0]
assert bad == 0
spark.sql("CALL catalog.system.fast_forward('prod.orders', 'main', 'etl_20260921')")
```

`fast_forward` is a metadata pointer move — readers on `main` see the old snapshot right up until it commits, then the new one. No partial state.

**On Snowflake or BigQuery**, the same shape without branches: build to a swap target, audit, then `ALTER TABLE prod.orders SWAP WITH prod.orders__wap;` (Snowflake's swap is atomic metadata) or write to `orders__wap` and re-point a view.

**In dbt**, `dbt build` already interleaves tests per model so a failing model blocks its children — but the *failing model itself* is still published. To get true WAP, materialize to an audit relation and publish in a `post-hook` gated on tests, or run the audit step in the orchestrator between two dbt invocations.

Three things people get wrong: the publish must be a metadata operation (a `CREATE OR REPLACE ... AS SELECT` re-reads and re-writes, widening the window again); the audit must query the unpublished version, or you have just tested last run's data; and you need a cleanup policy, since abandoned branches and `__wap` tables accumulate storage and confuse `SHOW TABLES`.

## 05:03 - [Data Analytics] Survival Curves for Churn: The Censored Users Your Retention Rate Is Guessing About

The usual churn number is `churned_users / total_users` over a window. It quietly assumes everyone in the denominator had an equal chance to churn. They didn't: a user who signed up three days before the window closed is counted as "retained" on the same footing as one who has been paying for two years. That's **right-censoring** — you know the user survived at least *t* days, not how long they'll actually last.

Both shortcuts are wrong. Drop censored users and you keep only the ones old enough to have churned, biasing retention down. Count them as permanent survivors and you bias it up. Kaplan-Meier handles them properly: each user contributes to the denominator only for as long as they were actually observed, then leaves.

The estimator is a running product over observed churn times: `S(t) = Π (1 − dᵢ / nᵢ)`, where `dᵢ` is churns at time *i* and `nᵢ` is the at-risk count — users neither churned nor censored before *i*.

Concretely, from 100 signups: month 1, 10 churn → S = 0.900. Month 2, 9 churn out of 90 at risk, and 20 users hit the analysis date with only two months of history → S = 0.810, and those 20 drop out of the denominator. Month 3, 6 churn out of the 61 still at risk → **S = 0.730**. Treat those 20 as survivors instead and month 3 reads 0.750 — two points of retention you invented.

Build the event table in SQL:

```sql
select u.user_id,
       date_diff('day', u.signup_date,
                 coalesce(c.cancel_date, current_date)) as tenure_days,
       (c.cancel_date is not null)::int as churned
from users u
left join cancellations c using (user_id)
```

Then KM in ~5 lines of pandas — no `lifelines` needed:

```python
t = (df.groupby("tenure_days")
       .agg(events=("churned", "sum"), exits=("churned", "size"))
       .sort_index())
t["at_risk"] = len(df) - t["exits"].cumsum().shift(fill_value=0)
t["survival"] = (1 - t["events"] / t["at_risk"]).cumprod()
```

Three traps. Censoring must be independent of churn — filtering to "users with 30+ days tenure" induces exactly the bias you're correcting. For cancel-at-period-end subscriptions the event date is the term end, not the click. And two curves that visually separate may not differ: use a log-rank test, since the tail is estimated from a handful of users and its confidence band is enormous.

## 10:03 - [ML & AI] UMAP and t-SNE: The Plot Is a Neighbor Graph, Not a Map

Both algorithms answer one question: *for each point, which points are its nearest neighbors?* They then arrange 2D positions so those neighborhoods survive. Everything else in the picture — the distance between two blobs, how tight a blob is, how big it looks — is a byproduct of the layout optimizer, not a fact about your data.

Three consequences people get burned by:

**Gap width is meaningless.** Two clusters 3 units apart and two clusters 30 units apart on a UMAP plot may be equally distinct in the original space. Neither algorithm has a loss term for preserving global distances.

**Cluster size is meaningless.** t-SNE in particular normalizes local density, so a tight 5,000-point cluster and a diffuse 50-point cluster can render at similar diameters.

**Noise produces clusters.** Sample 500 points from a 50-dimensional standard Gaussian — structureless by construction — and run UMAP at `n_neighbors=5`. You will get five or six crisp islands. The optimizer must place points somewhere, and with a small neighbor count it only ever sees local fragments. Raising `n_neighbors` to 100 collapses them back into one cloud. Run that control on your own data before believing a plot.

The rule that follows: **do not cluster on the 2D output.** Running k-means on UMAP coordinates clusters the layout's artifacts. Cluster in the original space (or on ~50 PCA components), then colour the embedding by those labels — the projection is a display surface, not a feature engineering step.

```python
from sklearn.decomposition import PCA
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
import umap

X50 = PCA(n_components=50).fit_transform(StandardScaler().fit_transform(X))
labels = KMeans(n_clusters=8, n_init=10).fit_predict(X50)   # cluster HERE

reducer = umap.UMAP(n_neighbors=30, min_dist=0.1, metric="cosine",
                    random_state=42)
emb = reducer.fit_transform(X50)                            # display only
plt.scatter(*emb.T, c=labels, s=4)
```

Knob semantics: `n_neighbors` is the local/global dial (15 is the default; 5 fragments, 200 globalizes). `min_dist` is purely cosmetic packing. t-SNE's `perplexity` is roughly "effective neighbors" and wants 5–50.

One operational difference decides production use: UMAP fits a reusable mapping, so `reducer.transform(X_new)` places new points in the same space. t-SNE has no out-of-sample extension — new data means refitting everything, and the axes change.

## 15:03 - [Crypto & Business] Business Idea: Bid Calibration Analytics for Regional Electrical Subcontractors

Most vertical-analytics ideas chase money already lost. This one closes a feedback loop that almost no mid-size contractor has closed.

**Customer.** Regional electrical or mechanical subcontractors, $15–50M revenue, 60–200 field staff, self-performing labor. Buyer is the owner or CFO; the daily user is the chief estimator.

**Problem.** They bid ~200 jobs a year and win ~25%. Estimates come out of Accubid/ConEst as labor units per item (hours per 100ft of 3/4" EMT, per device, per panel). Actuals land in Sage 300 CRE, Vista, or Foundation under cost codes. Nobody joins the two. So a labor unit that is 8% light stays 8% light for years.

The arithmetic is brutal. Take a $600k job bid at 4,200 labor hours × $68/hr loaded = $285,600. It runs 4,830 hours — $42,840 over, 7.1% of the job's contract value in a trade where net margin is 2–4%. One systematically bad unit repeated across 50 won jobs is the entire year's profit.

**Product & money.** Read-only monthly export of estimate + job cost. Map estimating items to cost codes, produce per-item estimate-vs-actual variance ranked by dollar impact, plus a recalibration sheet the estimator can load back into Accubid. $2,000–3,500/month. At $24k/year it pays for itself by correcting one recurring unit.

**Startup cost.** Genuinely low: ~$5–10k. A laptop, DuckDB or a $20/month Postgres, and the real expense — GL plus E&O insurance, roughly $1,800/year, because you are touching numbers people bid on.

**Main risk.** Not the analytics. It's the mapping: estimating item codes and accounting cost codes are one-to-many and idiosyncratic per company, so customers one through three each take three weeks of manual mapping and it productizes far slower than the pitch deck says. Secondary risk is political — estimators read a variance report as a performance review. Sell it to the CFO, position it to the estimator as a calibration tool, never a scorecard.

**First validation step.** Build nothing. Get one contractor to email estimate exports and job-cost reports for 10 closed jobs. Spend a weekend mapping them in a spreadsheet. If no recurring directional bias worth >1% of labor cost shows up, the thesis is dead for $0. If it does, charge $3,500 for that same one-off report three more times before writing a line of product code.

## 19:03 - [PC Hardware] Thermal Paste: Pump-Out, Mounting Pressure, and When a Repaste Fixes Nothing

Most "my temps got worse after a year" posts are not dried-out paste. They are **pump-out**: the cyclical expansion and contraction of the CPU IHS and cooler baseplate as the chip heats and cools, which slowly squeezes paste out of the center of the contact patch and leaves voids. Thin, low-viscosity pastes pump out fastest. Thick ones resist it but need more mounting pressure to spread.

Two hardware factors matter more than which tube you buy:

**IHS flatness.** Intel LGA1700/1851 sockets use an integrated loading mechanism that bends the IHS concave by roughly 0.05 mm under load, so the cooler contacts the edges and starves the center — exactly where the hot cores sit. An aftermarket **contact frame** (Thermalright LGA1700-BCF and similar) replaces the stock ILM and typically drops package temps 4–8 °C under sustained all-core load. That is a bigger win than any paste upgrade. AM5's IHS is thick and mostly flat; contact frames there buy ~1–2 °C and are not worth the risk.

**Application pattern.** For a modern rectangular IHS, a single center pea spreads poorly along the long axis. A small center dot plus four smaller dots, or a thin line along the long axis, covers better. Do not spread it manually with a card unless you are careful about air entrapment. Quantity: about a grain of rice for a 35×35 mm desktop IHS.

Expected gains, roughly:

| Change | Typical Δ under sustained load |
|---|---|
| Cheap bundled paste → good paste (MX-6, Kryonaut) | 2–4 °C |
| Good paste → PTM7950 phase-change pad | 0–3 °C, but no pump-out for years |
| Stock ILM → contact frame (LGA1700) | 4–8 °C |
| Any paste → liquid metal (delidded/direct-die) | 5–15 °C, with real spill and galvanic risk |

**When a repaste fixes nothing:** if your idle delta over ambient is normal (~10–15 °C) but load temps spike instantly to 95 °C, that is power limit behavior, not a mount problem. Check PL1/PL2 or PPT first. Repaste when idle delta has crept up 10 °C+ from where it started, or every 2–3 years on a heavily cycled machine. Phase-change pads make that interval effectively irrelevant.
