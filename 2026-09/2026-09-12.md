## 00:03 - [Data Engineering] Airflow Concurrency: Why Your Task Sits in `queued` Forever

A task stuck in `queued` is not a broken worker — it means the scheduler already decided the task is runnable and something is throttling it. Airflow enforces **four independent limits**, and the tightest one wins. Debugging means finding which.

**1. Deployment-wide: `parallelism`** (default 32) caps running task instances across the entire scheduler.

```ini
# airflow.cfg
[core]
parallelism = 128
max_active_tasks_per_dag = 16
max_active_runs_per_dag = 16
```

**2. Per-DAG task limit: `max_active_tasks`** — even with 500 idle worker slots, one DAG runs at most this many tasks.

**3. Per-DAG run limit: `max_active_runs`** — the silent backfill killer. At `max_active_runs=1`, a 90-day backfill runs strictly serially no matter how wide your cluster is.

```python
with DAG(
    "orders_etl",
    schedule="@daily",
    max_active_tasks=32,
    max_active_runs=4,          # allow 4 logical dates in flight
    catchup=True,
) as dag:
    ...
```

**4. Pools** — the one people forget. Every task lands in `default_pool` (128 slots) unless assigned. Pools are how you protect a fragile upstream:

```python
extract = PythonOperator(
    task_id="extract_from_oracle",
    python_callable=extract,
    pool="oracle_legacy",   # pool created with 3 slots
    pool_slots=1,           # a heavy Spark task might claim 4
)
```

Now at most three tasks hit that database concurrently, cluster-wide, across every DAG.

**Two task-level limits worth knowing:** `max_active_tis_per_dag` caps copies of *one task* across all runs — essential when backfilling a task that writes to a shared partition. `max_active_tis_per_dagrun` does the same within a single run's mapped tasks.

**Diagnosing:** `scheduled` means Airflow hasn't picked it; `queued` means it's gated or waiting on an executor slot. Check Admin → Pools for used vs. queued slots, then work outward:

```sql
SELECT pool, state, count(*)
FROM task_instance
WHERE state IN ('queued','running')
GROUP BY 1,2;
```

If a pool shows 0 free slots, that's your answer. If pools look fine, you're hitting `parallelism` or Celery's `worker_concurrency`. Raising `parallelism` without raising worker capacity just moves the queue — size limits to what your *warehouse* tolerates, not what your scheduler can emit.

## 05:03 - [Data Analytics] Simpson's Paradox: Every Segment Improved and the Total Still Fell

You present Q2 conversion. Desktop is up. Mobile is up. Overall conversion dropped 1.4 points. Someone asks which number is wrong. Neither is.

| Quarter | Segment | Visits | Conv. | Rate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Q1 | desktop | 900 | 90 | 10.0% |
| Q1 | mobile | 100 | 4 | 4.0% |
| **Q1** | **all** | **1000** | **94** | **9.4%** |
| Q2 | desktop | 400 | 44 | 11.0% |
| Q2 | mobile | 600 | 36 | 6.0% |
| **Q2** | **all** | **1000** | **80** | **8.0%** |

The overall rate is a *weighted average* of segment rates, and the weights moved: mobile went from 10% of traffic to 60%, and mobile converts worse. A marketing campaign shifted the traffic mix, and the mix swamped the genuine improvement inside each segment.

### Decompose instead of arguing

The change splits exactly into three terms (`w` = share of visits, `r` = segment rate):

`ΔR = Σ Δw·r₀ (mix) + Σ w₀·Δr (rate) + Σ Δw·Δr (interaction)`

```sql
WITH quarterly(quarter, device, visits, conversions) AS (
    VALUES ('Q1','desktop',900,90), ('Q1','mobile',100, 4),
           ('Q2','desktop',400,44), ('Q2','mobile',600,36)
),
seg AS (
    SELECT quarter, device,
           conversions * 1.0 / visits                            AS rate,
           visits * 1.0 / SUM(visits) OVER (PARTITION BY quarter) AS weight
    FROM quarterly
),
paired AS (
    SELECT a.weight AS w0, b.weight AS w1, a.rate AS r0, b.rate AS r1
    FROM seg a JOIN seg b ON a.device = b.device
    WHERE a.quarter = 'Q1' AND b.quarter = 'Q2'
)
SELECT ROUND(SUM((w1-w0) *  r0     ) * 100, 2) AS mix_pp,
       ROUND(SUM( w0     * (r1-r0) ) * 100, 2) AS rate_pp,
       ROUND(SUM((w1-w0) * (r1-r0) ) * 100, 2) AS interaction_pp
FROM paired;
-- mix_pp = -3.00 | rate_pp = +1.10 | interaction_pp = +0.50  →  sums to -1.40
```

The three terms sum to the actual change with no residual — it's an algebraic identity, not an approximation. Because segment weights always sum to 1, `Σ Δw = 0`, which is what makes the mix term interpretable on its own.

**In practice:** when the total and every segment disagree, mix is doing the work — lead with that, don't bury it. Report the decomposition rather than silently "controlling for" device, because the mix shift is usually the real business event. Two caveats: the paradox can only be detected on dimensions you actually collected, and a different split (channel, geo, new vs. returning) can flip the story again, so decompose on the dimension you believe drives acquisition — not the one that makes the number look best.

## 10:02 - [ML & AI] Shadow Deployments: What Mirroring Traffic Proves, and What It Can't

A shadow deployment sends live production requests to a new model alongside the current one and throws the new model's answer away. Nothing user-facing changes. It is the cheapest pre-launch test you have, and it is routinely over-trusted.

**What it genuinely proves.** That the challenger *runs*: no missing feature, no dtype mismatch, no unseen category exploding the encoder. That its features resolve identically in the serving path — shadow mode is the only place training/serving skew shows up under real request shapes. And that its tail latency survives production load, which offline benchmarks on a quiet box never tell you.

**The number that matters is disagreement, not accuracy.** Say 100k requests/day, champion flags 1.5% for review:

| | count |
|---|---|
| champion flags | 1,500 |
| challenger flags | 1,900 |
| both agree (flagged) | 1,200 |
| champion-only | 300 |
| challenger-only | 700 |

Overall disagreement is 1,000/100,000 = **1.0%** — reassuringly tiny, and meaningless. The honest framing: 20% of the champion's flags disappear, and the review queue grows 27%. Shadow mode sizes the operational blast radius before anyone feels it.

```python
async def handle(req):
    task = asyncio.create_task(champion.predict(req))
    asyncio.create_task(shadow(req))        # fire-and-forget, never awaited
    return await task

async def shadow(req):
    try:
        async with asyncio.timeout(0.25):   # must not outlive the real request
            log.emit({"id": req.id, "pred": await challenger.predict(req),
                      "version": CHALLENGER_VERSION})
    except Exception:
        metrics.incr("shadow.error")        # count it, never raise
```

Swallowing shadow errors is required — *not* counting them defeats the point, since a silent 8% failure rate is exactly what you deployed shadow mode to find.

**The hard limit: shadow mode cannot measure whether the model is better.** Outcome labels depend on actions the shadow never took. A shadowed fraud model blocks nothing, so the transactions it would have declined still complete, and the ones the champion declined have no outcome at all. You're comparing scores against labels generated under the champion's policy.

Shadow answers "is this safe to serve?" Only a canary or interleaved A/B — where the challenger's decisions actually take effect — answers "is this better?" Run shadow first; it's a gate, not a verdict.

## 15:02 - [Crypto & Business] DeFi Lending Liquidations: Health Factor, Liquidation Price, and the 3% Trap

Overcollateralized lending (Aave, Compound, Morpho) has no credit check, so the protocol's only defense is seizing collateral before the debt goes underwater. Three parameters govern this, and people routinely confuse the first two.

- **Max LTV** — how much you're *allowed* to borrow at open (ETH: ~80%).
- **Liquidation threshold** — where liquidation becomes legal (ETH: ~82.5%). The gap between these is your entire safety buffer.
- **Liquidation penalty/bonus** — what a liquidator keeps for doing the work (~5%).

**Health factor** = (collateral × liquidation threshold) ÷ debt. Below 1.0, you're liquidatable.

**Worked example.** Supply 10 ETH at $3,000 = $30,000. Borrow $18,000 USDC.

HF = (30,000 × 0.825) ÷ 18,000 = **1.375**

Solve for the ETH price where HF = 1: 10 × P × 0.825 = 18,000 → **P = $2,182**, a 27% drop.

Now borrow the max $24,000 instead: P = 24,000 ÷ 8.25 = **$2,909**. That's a **3% move** from spot. This is the trap — max LTV feels like permission, but it puts liquidation inside normal intraday noise.

**The liquidation itself.** A liquidator repays up to 50% of your debt (100% if HF < 0.95) and receives that value in collateral plus the bonus. Repay $9,000, seize $9,450 of ETH — you eat the $450 as a pure loss, on top of the price move.

**Two things that kill positions quietly:**

1. **Interest drift.** At 6% borrow APR, $18,000 becomes $19,080 in a year with zero price movement. HF falls 1.375 → 1.297; liquidation price rises $2,182 → $2,313.
2. **Oracle lag.** Liquidation triggers on the protocol's oracle price, not the exchange chart you're watching. During crashes, gas also spikes — the moment you most need to add collateral is the moment the transaction is most expensive and slowest to land.

Practical discipline: size for a 50%+ drawdown (HF ≥ 1.8 on volatile collateral), and remember that correlated collateral like stETH/ETH reduces divergence risk but not price risk.

## 19:02 - [PC Hardware] Laptop GPUs: Why Two Machines With the Same "RTX 4070" Differ by 40%

On desktop, a GPU model number is a contract — a 4070 is a 4070. On laptops it is closer to a marketing category. The same silicon ships across a wide **TGP (Total Graphics Power)** range set by the OEM, and power, not the model name, decides performance.

NVIDIA's published ranges for the 40-series laptop parts:

| Laptop GPU | TGP range | Dynamic Boost |
|---|---|---|
| RTX 4060 | 35–115 W | +15 W |
| RTX 4070 | 35–115 W | +15 W |
| RTX 4080 | 60–150 W | +25 W |
| RTX 4090 | 80–150 W | +25 W |

A 4070 in a 16 mm thin-and-light at 35 W routinely loses to a 4060 at 115 W in a thicker chassis. Spending up a tier buys nothing if you spend it into a worse cooler. NVIDIA has required OEMs to publish TGP on product pages since 2021 — if a listing hides it, treat that as the answer.

**Three more things the model number doesn't tell you:**

- **Shared power budget.** CPU and GPU draw from one thermal/power envelope. A laptop advertising 45 W CPU + 115 W GPU will not sustain both at once; under a combined load the firmware throttles one. This is why gaming benchmarks and rendering benchmarks rank laptops differently.
- **Display routing.** With Optimus, frames render on the dGPU then pass through the iGPU to reach the panel — costing roughly 5–15% of framerate and adding latency. A **MUX switch** or Advanced Optimus lets you bind the panel directly to the dGPU. Check the spec sheet; on Windows verify in the NVIDIA control panel's display section.
- **Sustained vs. burst.** Reviews that report a single 3-minute run measure PL2 boost. What you live with is the 20-minute steady state. Look for reviews that publish a sustained loop and 1% lows, not just an average.

**Before buying, check in this order:** published TGP → chassis thickness and cooler design → whether RAM is soldered (LPDDR5X) or upgradeable (SO-DIMM/CAMM2) → MUX switch → panel spec.

On Linux, confirm what you actually have:

```bash
nvidia-smi -q -d POWER | grep -E 'Power Limit|Power Draw'
nvidia-smi --query-gpu=name,power.max_limit --format=csv
```

If `Max Power Limit` reads 80 W on a machine sold as a 4080 laptop, that is the whole story.
