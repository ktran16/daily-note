## 00:03 - [Data Engineering] Spark AQE: Why Tuning `shuffle.partitions` Backwards Makes Jobs Faster

The old Spark tuning ritual was to guess `spark.sql.shuffle.partitions` — too high and you get thousands of 2 MB tasks with scheduler overhead dominating; too low and tasks spill to disk. Adaptive Query Execution (on by default since Spark 3.2) inverts the advice: set it **high** and let Spark coalesce down.

AQE re-plans at shuffle boundaries using real `MapOutputStatistics`, not estimates. Three optimizations matter:

```python
# These are defaults in Spark 3.2+ — shown to make the knobs visible
spark.conf.set("spark.sql.adaptive.enabled", "true")
spark.conf.set("spark.sql.adaptive.coalescePartitions.enabled", "true")
spark.conf.set("spark.sql.adaptive.advisoryPartitionSizeInBytes", "64MB")
spark.conf.set("spark.sql.adaptive.skewJoin.enabled", "true")

# Counter-intuitive: make this LARGE. It is now only the *initial* count.
spark.conf.set("spark.sql.shuffle.partitions", "2000")
```

1. **Coalescing.** 2000 initial partitions holding 8 GB get merged into ~128 tasks of ~64 MB each. A filter that drops 99% of rows no longer produces 2000 nearly-empty tasks.
2. **Join strategy switching.** A sort-merge join whose build side turns out to be 30 MB after filtering is rewritten into a broadcast hash join mid-flight — something static planning misses because it only sees pre-filter table stats.
3. **Skew join splitting.** A partition larger than both `skewedPartitionThresholdInBytes` (256 MB) and `skewedPartitionFactor` × median (5×) is split into sub-partitions, with the other side replicated.

The traps:

- **AQE only re-optimizes at shuffle boundaries.** A scan → filter → write job has no exchange, so AQE does nothing. Skew there is a file-layout problem.
- **Skew splitting covers joins, not aggregations.** A `GROUP BY` on a key where one value is 60% of rows still has a straggler — salt it manually.
- **Setting `shuffle.partitions` low defeats AQE.** Coalescing can merge partitions, never split them. Leaving the old hand-tuned `200` caps how fine-grained AQE can get.

Confirm it fired: the Spark UI SQL tab shows `AQEShuffleRead` nodes labeled `coalesced` or `skewed`, and the final plan differs from the initial one.

## 05:03 - [Data Analytics] Rolling-Origin Backtesting: Why Shuffled K-Fold Says Your Forecast Is 1.8x Better Than It Is

`KFold(shuffle=True)` is the default reflex for validation, and on a time series it quietly answers the wrong question. Shuffling puts test days *between* training days, so the model only has to interpolate — but a forecast has to extrapolate past the last row it ever saw. Any feature carrying a monotone level (a date index, `cumulative_signups`, an autoincrement id) turns that gap into a fantasy score.

Two years of trending, weekly-seasonal daily revenue, a random forest on `day` and `dow`:

```python
import numpy as np, pandas as pd
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestRegressor
from sklearn.model_selection import KFold, TimeSeriesSplit
from sklearn.metrics import mean_absolute_error

rng = np.random.default_rng(7); n = 730; t = np.arange(n)
y = 1000 + 2.0*t + 150*np.sin(2*np.pi*t/7) + rng.normal(0, 80, n)
df = pd.DataFrame({"day": t, "dow": t % 7, "y": y})
X, yv = df[["day", "dow"]].values, df.y.values

def cv(sp):
    e = [mean_absolute_error(yv[te],
         RandomForestRegressor(n_estimators=200, random_state=0)
             .fit(X[tr], yv[tr]).predict(X[te]))
         for tr, te in sp.split(X)]
    return np.mean(e)

print(cv(KFold(5, shuffle=True, random_state=0)))  #  70.5
print(cv(TimeSeriesSplit(n_splits=5)))             # 165.9
```

Train on the first 640 days and forecast the last 90 — the thing you actually ship — and MAE is **124.8**. Shuffled K-fold promised 70.5: off by 1.8x, and off in the flattering direction. Trees cannot predict above their training range, so every forecast beyond day 640 flatlines while the real trend keeps climbing. K-fold never tested that because it always had a future row in the training set.

`TimeSeriesSplit` reported 165.9 — pessimistic, because its first folds train on only ~120 days. That is the right kind of wrong: it errs toward caution instead of selling you a model. Read the per-fold spread (173, 149, 149, 155, 204), not just the mean; a fold that blows up is usually one regime change.

Two rules that follow:

- **Size the test fold like your real horizon.** Validating one-day-ahead when the business forecasts a quarter measures a different model.
- **Build features from the past only.** `y.shift(1).rolling(7).mean()` is a feature; `y.rolling(7, center=True).mean()` reads three days into the future and will not exist at prediction time. For multi-step horizons, add a gap between train and test so lag features can't straddle the boundary.

## 10:03 - [ML & AI] Agent Loops Are Quadratic: You Process 650K Tokens to End at 61K

Every agentic tool-use loop resends the entire conversation on every step. People budget the system prompt and the final context size, then get a bill that makes no sense. The gap is arithmetic, and it is quadratic.

### The arithmetic

Let `p` = fixed prefix (system prompt + tool schemas) and `r` = tokens one step appends (the `tool_use` block + its `tool_result` + assistant text). Step *n* sends `p + r·(n-1)` input tokens, so across *N* steps:

> total input processed ≈ **N·p + r · N(N-1)/2**

With `p = 4,000`, `r = 3,000`, `N = 20`:

| Quantity | Tokens |
|---|---|
| Final context at step 20 | 4,000 + 3,000×19 = **61,000** |
| Total input *processed* | 80,000 + 3,000×190 = **650,000** |

You paid for 650K input to arrive at a 61K conversation — **10.7×** the number you were budgeting. Double the steps and the ratio roughly doubles again.

### Where `r` actually goes

Tool results dominate. A `grep` returning 2,000 lines is ~25K tokens; one careless result sets `r` an order of magnitude higher than the model's own output and gets resent every remaining step. Three fixes, in order of payoff:

**1. Cap results at the tool boundary, not in the prompt.**

```python
MAX_CHARS = 8_000  # ~2K tokens; verify with the count_tokens endpoint

def bounded(text: str, cursor: int = 0) -> dict:
    chunk = text[cursor : cursor + MAX_CHARS]
    nxt = cursor + len(chunk)
    return {
        "content": chunk,
        "truncated": nxt < len(text),
        "next_cursor": nxt if nxt < len(text) else None,
        "total_chars": len(text),
    }
```

Give the model a cursor and it fetches more only when it needs more.

**2. Make errors one line, not a traceback.** Return `is_error: True` with `"file not found: cfg.yml; try list_files()"`. A 400-line stack trace costs ~5K tokens *per remaining step* and tells the model less than one actionable sentence.

**3. Keep history append-only so prompt caching applies.** The prefix is identical across steps, so cache reads collapse most of that quadratic term to a fraction of base input price. Any byte edited in the prefix invalidates everything after it — rewriting or re-summarizing earlier turns in place forfeits the whole discount. When context genuinely must shrink, use server-side context editing (clear old tool results) or compaction rather than hand-editing the array.

**Rule of thumb:** cap any single tool result at ~2K tokens, cap retries per tool at 2, and treat `r` as the number you tune — not `N`.

## 15:05 - [Crypto & Business] Business Idea: Workers' Comp X-Mod Audits for Mid-Size Staffing Agencies and Trade Contractors

**Customer.** US staffing agencies and specialty trade contractors with $4–15M in annual payroll in light-industrial, framing, roofing, or mechanical class codes. Big enough to be experience-rated, too small for a risk manager.

**The pain.** Workers' comp premium = payroll ÷ 100 × class rate × **experience modification factor**. The mod is computed by a rating bureau (NCCI in ~38 states) from three years of payroll and claims, skipping the most recent year — so a bad year taxes you for three. Claim *reserves* are valuated 18 months after policy inception and reported on a unit statistical report. A knee claim reserved at $85K that later settles for $11K stays in the mod at $85K unless someone files a USR correction before the window closes.

**Worked example.** $6M payroll, blended rate $4.50/$100 → $270K manual premium. Mod 1.28 → $345K. Three corrections — stale reserves on two closed claims, a subrogation recovery never credited, and overtime premium (the extra third of time-and-a-half, excludable in most states when payroll records separate it) — bring the mod to 1.06 → $286K. **$59K/year**, and it compounds across the three-year window. Second value driver: many GCs and owners won't let you bid with a mod above 1.00, so the audit buys access to work, not just savings.

**Revenue.** 25–30% contingency on verified year-one premium reduction, or a $7.5K audit plus a $1.5K/mo monitoring retainer that re-runs the mod every time a reserve moves.

**Startup cost.** ~$12–20K: rating-bureau manual access for expected loss rates, D-ratios, and ballast/weight tables ($3–6K/yr), E&O insurance and entity ($3K), minimal cloud. The build is a loss-run/USR parser plus a recalculation engine that prices each claim's marginal mod impact.

**Main risk.** The incumbent broker does a free, shallow version of this. Your wedge is their conflict — broker commission is a percentage of premium, so cutting premium cuts their pay. Secondary risk: some states restrict who may advise on insurance. Position as data analytics and get counsel before selling.

**First validation step.** Through a state staffing association, get three agencies' five-year loss runs and USRs. Recalculate by hand. If two of three show a correctable error worth over $15K, you have a business; do those free for references.
