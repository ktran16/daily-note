## 00:03 - [Data Engineering] The Transactional Outbox: Why Dual Writes Lose Events

Your service saves an order and publishes `order.created` to Kafka. Two systems, one request, no shared transaction:

```python
db.commit()                      # succeeded
producer.send("orders", event)   # process dies here
```

The order exists and no downstream system ever hears about it. Reverse the order and you get the opposite failure: an event for an order that rolled back. There is no ordering of these two calls that is safe — this is the **dual-write problem**, and retries don't fix it, because the crash can land between the commit and the retry loop.

**The fix: make the event part of the transaction.** Write it to an outbox table in the same database, in the same commit as the business change.

```sql
CREATE TABLE outbox (
  id            uuid PRIMARY KEY DEFAULT gen_random_uuid(),
  aggregate_type text NOT NULL,   -- 'order'      -> topic routing
  aggregate_id   text NOT NULL,   -- '1042'       -> partition key
  event_type     text NOT NULL,   -- 'order.created'
  payload        jsonb NOT NULL,
  created_at     timestamptz NOT NULL DEFAULT now()
);

BEGIN;
  INSERT INTO orders (id, customer_id, total) VALUES (1042, 77, 250.00);
  INSERT INTO outbox (aggregate_type, aggregate_id, event_type, payload)
  VALUES ('order', '1042', 'order.created',
          '{"order_id":1042,"customer_id":77,"total":250.00}');
COMMIT;
```

One atomic commit: either both rows land or neither does. A separate relay then tails the outbox and publishes. Use log-based CDC (Debezium reading the WAL), not a polling `SELECT ... WHERE published = false` loop — polling adds lag and contention:

```json
"transforms": "outbox",
"transforms.outbox.type": "io.debezium.transforms.outbox.EventRouter",
"transforms.outbox.route.by.field": "aggregate_type",
"transforms.outbox.table.field.event.key": "aggregate_id"
```

**Three things to get right:**

- **Consumers must be idempotent.** The relay guarantees at-least-once, so dedupe on `id` — the row's UUID is a natural idempotency key.
- **Ordering is per-key only.** Keying by `aggregate_id` keeps one order's events in sequence; there is no global order.
- **Prune the table.** Delete rows older than your CDC retention window on a schedule. In Postgres you can even `INSERT` then `DELETE` in the same transaction — logical decoding still emits the insert from the WAL, so the table stays empty.

**Why not just CDC the `orders` table directly?** Because that publishes your internal schema. Rename a column and every consumer breaks. The outbox payload is a deliberate, versioned contract you control, decoupled from how you happen to store the data today.

## 05:02 - [Data Analytics] UNPIVOT and the NULLs It Silently Drops

Pivoting wide-to-long looks like pure reshaping, so nobody audits it. But `UNPIVOT` in Oracle and Snowflake defaults to `EXCLUDE NULLS`, and SQL Server's drops them with **no option to keep them**. Rows don't just move — they disappear.

Take a 1,000-respondent survey. Everyone answered `q1_ease` and `q2_speed`; only 400 answered `q3_support`.

```sql
-- Snowflake / Oracle
SELECT respondent_id, question, score
FROM responses
UNPIVOT (score FOR question IN (q1_ease, q2_speed, q3_support));
```

You expect 3,000 rows. You get 2,400. Now the dashboard:

```sql
SELECT question,
       AVG(score)   AS avg_score,
       COUNT(*)     AS n,
       COUNT(*) / 1000.0 AS response_rate
FROM unpivoted GROUP BY question;
```

`avg_score` is fine — `AVG` ignores NULLs anyway. The damage is in the counts. `q3_support` reports `n = 400` and a **100% response rate** if your denominator comes from the unpivoted row count rather than the respondent roster. The 600 non-responses aren't underrepresented; they're gone. Any completeness check downstream passes, because every row that survived has a value.

Two fixes:

```sql
-- 1. Keep them explicitly (Snowflake, Oracle)
UNPIVOT INCLUDE NULLS (score FOR question IN (q1_ease, q2_speed, q3_support))
```

```sql
-- 2. Portable: LATERAL VALUES, which preserves every row
SELECT r.respondent_id, v.question, v.score
FROM responses r
CROSS JOIN LATERAL (VALUES
    ('q1_ease',    r.q1_ease),
    ('q2_speed',   r.q2_speed),
    ('q3_support', r.q3_support)
) AS v(question, score);
```

Option 2 works in Postgres (which has no `UNPIVOT`), is dialect-stable, and makes the NULL handling visible in the query text instead of hiding it in a keyword default. `UNION ALL` of three `SELECT`s is equally safe but rescans the table per branch.

**The guardrail:** assert `row_count = respondents × questions` right after any unpivot. A reshape that changes your row count is a filter wearing a reshape's clothing — and the direction of the error always flatters you, because missing data is what vanishes.

## 10:03 - [ML & AI] Random Search Beats Grid Search, and Log-Uniform Beats Both

A 4-hyperparameter grid with 5 values each is 625 fits. At 90 seconds a fit with 5-fold CV, that's 16 hours. The usual reaction is to cut the grid to 3 values each — 81 fits — which is worse than it looks.

**Why grid search wastes its budget.** In almost every real model, one or two hyperparameters dominate and the rest barely move the score. A 5×5×5×5 grid tries exactly **5 distinct values** of the parameter that matters, spending 625 fits to do it. 625 random draws try 625 distinct values of it. Grid search's resolution on the axis you care about is set by the axes you don't.

The budget math (Bergstra & Bengio): if the top 5% of the search space is "good enough," then `n` independent random draws miss it with probability `0.95^n`.

```
n = 20  ->  1 - 0.95^20 = 64%
n = 60  ->  1 - 0.95^60 = 95.4%
n = 90  ->  1 - 0.95^90 = 99.0%
```

60 trials gets you a top-5% config 95% of the time, in **one tenth** the compute of the grid — and the result is independent of how many irrelevant dimensions you added.

**Now the part people get wrong: the distribution.** `uniform(0.001, 0.3)` for a learning rate puts only ~3% of draws below 0.01, where boosted trees actually live. Learning rates, regularization strengths, and `C` are scale-parameters — sample them log-uniformly:

```python
from scipy.stats import loguniform, randint
from sklearn.model_selection import RandomizedSearchCV

search = RandomizedSearchCV(
    LGBMClassifier(n_estimators=2000),
    {"learning_rate":     loguniform(1e-3, 3e-1),   # 40% of draws below 0.01
     "reg_lambda":        loguniform(1e-3, 1e2),
     "num_leaves":        randint(8, 256),
     "min_child_samples": randint(5, 200)},
    n_iter=60, cv=5, scoring="average_precision",
    random_state=0, n_jobs=-1,
)
```

Over `[1e-3, 0.3]` that's 2.48 decades, so a full 40% of log-uniform draws land under 0.01 instead of 3%.

**When fits are expensive**, graduate to `HalvingRandomSearchCV` (kill bad configs on small budgets) or Optuna's TPE with pruning — both reallocate compute toward promising regions rather than sampling blindly.

**The guardrail:** `search.best_score_` is the maximum over 60 noisy estimates, so it is biased upward by roughly the CV standard error. Report the score on a held-out set the search never touched.

## 15:02 - [Crypto & Business] Finding Your ICP in Data You Already Have: Win Rate, Cycle Length, and Churn by Segment

Most teams write their ideal customer profile (ICP) from ambition — "mid-market companies that care about data quality." A real ICP is an empirical finding. You already have the data: your closed-won, closed-lost, and churned records. Cut them by one candidate segment at a time and look at four numbers together.

**Worked example.** A $900/mo analytics tool pulls 18 months of pipeline and splits by the buyer's job function:

| Segment | Deals | Win rate | Median cycle | 12-mo logo churn | CAC | Gross margin/yr |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ops / RevOps | 140 | 31% | 34 days | 9% | $4,100 | $8,600 |
| Finance | 95 | 22% | 61 days | 14% | $6,900 | $8,600 |
| Engineering | 180 | 12% | 88 days | 31% | $12,400 | $8,600 |

Engineering is the biggest source of volume and the reason the company feels busy. It is also unprofitable: $12,400 CAC against $8,600 of annual gross profit is a **17-month payback** on a cohort losing a third of its logos inside 12 months — most of those accounts never repay acquisition cost. RevOps pays back in **5.7 months** and keeps 91%.

**Why the four numbers must be read jointly.** High win rate alone can mean you are discounting. Low churn alone can mean a tiny, slow-growing niche. Short cycles with high churn signal impulse buyers. Payback (CAC ÷ monthly gross profit) and retention together are what decide whether a segment compounds.

**Practical cautions.** Require ~30+ deals per cell or you are reading noise. Watch for confounds — if all engineering deals came from one bad channel, you have a channel problem, not a segment problem; cross-tab segment × source. And segments defined by *observable* traits (industry, employee count, tech stack, job title) are usable in targeting; "data-driven culture" is not.

**The action:** reallocate spend and qualification criteria toward the winning cell, then re-measure in two quarters. Narrowing ICP usually raises win rate and lowers CAC simultaneously.
