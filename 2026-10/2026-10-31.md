## 00:03 - [Data Engineering] Airflow Asset Scheduling: The Trigger Fires on Task Success, Not on Data

Data-aware scheduling (Datasets in Airflow 2.4+, renamed **Assets** in 3.0) lets a consumer DAG run when a producer declares it updated something, instead of guessing at a cron offset:

```python
from airflow.sdk import Asset, DAG, task

orders = Asset("s3://warehouse/orders/")

with DAG("load_orders", schedule="0 * * * *"):
    @task(outlets=[orders])
    def load():
        ...  # write the files

with DAG("build_marts", schedule=[orders]):  # no cron at all
    ...
```

Two things about this surprise people in production.

**The event is emitted on task success, not on data arrival.** Airflow has no idea what your task wrote. A task that fetched zero rows, hit an empty source, or short-circuited past the write still returns success, still emits the asset event, and still triggers everything downstream — which then rebuilds marts on yesterday's data. Make the emission conditional on the write actually happening:

```python
@task(outlets=[orders])
def load(**context):
    rows = write_orders()
    if rows == 0:
        raise AirflowSkipException("nothing landed; not signalling orders")
    context["outlet_events"][orders].extra = {"rows": rows}
```

A skipped or failed task does not emit. Downstream stays put, which is what you want.

**Multi-asset schedules are "all, at least once" — and updates collapse.** `schedule=[a, b]` means *every* asset has been updated at least once since the last run (AND). If `a` updates five times while `b` lags, you get **one** run, not five: there is no queue and no per-partition alignment. Use conditional expressions when AND isn't the rule:

```python
with DAG("build_marts", schedule=(orders | returns) & dim_customers):
    ...
```

And pair with a timetable when you also need a floor:

```python
from airflow.timetables.assets import AssetOrTimeSchedule
from airflow.timetables.trigger import CronTriggerTimetable

schedule=AssetOrTimeSchedule(
    timetable=CronTriggerTimetable("0 6 * * *", timezone="UTC"),
    assets=orders,
)
```

**Practical rule:** treat an asset as a *signal*, not a *guarantee*. Keep the correctness check in the consumer — a freshness assertion or a `max(loaded_at)` watermark guard — because the only thing the event proves is that some upstream task exited zero.

## 05:05 - [Data Analytics] Categorical Color: Audit the Palette, Don't Trust Its Reputation

`tab10`, Tableau 10, and the Plotly default all carry a vague reputation for being colorblind-safe. Measure one and the reputation collapses. Simulate deuteranomaly, then compute perceptual distance between every pair:

```python
from itertools import combinations
import matplotlib.colors as mc
from colorspacious import cspace_convert, deltaE

CVD = {"name": "sRGB1+CVD", "cvd_type": "deuteranomaly", "severity": 100}

def audit(palette, threshold=15):
    sim = cspace_convert([mc.to_rgb(c) for c in palette], CVD, "sRGB1").clip(0, 1)
    pairs = []
    for (i, a), (j, b) in combinations(enumerate(sim), 2):
        d = deltaE(a, b, input_space="sRGB1", uniform_space="CAM02-UCS")
        if d < threshold:
            pairs.append((palette[i], palette[j], round(float(d), 1)))
    return sorted(pairs, key=lambda r: r[2])
```

Run it on `tab10` and 5 of the 45 pairs land under 15:

```
('#e377c2', '#17becf', 4.3)   pink / cyan
('#ff7f0e', '#bcbd22', 4.5)   orange / olive
('#2ca02c', '#d62728', 5.2)   green / red
('#1f77b4', '#9467bd', 6.8)   blue / purple
('#8c564b', '#7f7f7f', 14.4)  brown / grey
```

Two of those collisions are inside the **first five** colors, so "I only plotted five series" doesn't save you. For roughly 8% of men of Northern European ancestry, your green and red lines are the same line. Okabe–Ito's 8-color set, by contrast, has a worst pair of 14.8 — effectively clean at eight categories.

The second, quieter failure is luminance. `tab10`'s lightness runs 45.8 to 76.8 in CAM02-UCS, with green at 59.0 and red at 52.8. Photocopy that chart, screenshot it into a grayscale deck, or view it on an e-ink reader and those two are six points apart out of a 31-point range.

**What to do instead:**

- Cap series at 5–6 plus an explicit `Other` bucket. A 12-hue legend is 12 lookups per glance regardless of palette.
- Direct-label lines at their right edge and delete the legend entirely — zero lookups, and color becomes decoration rather than the sole channel.
- Hue encodes *identity* only. Ordered data gets a single-hue luminance ramp; nobody reads "purple > teal" as a magnitude.
- Reserve red and green for good/bad semantics. Spending them on `EMEA` and `APAC` burns your only pre-attentive signal.
- Redundantly encode in line charts: dash pattern or marker shape survives both CVD and grayscale.

Treat 15 as a smell, not a verdict — eyeball flagged pairs at the mark size you actually ship. A 2px sparkline needs far more separation than a bar.

## 10:03 - [ML & AI] LightGBM Grows Leaf-Wise: Why `num_leaves = 2**max_depth` Overfits

XGBoost grows trees **level-wise**: split every node at depth *d* before touching depth *d+1*. The tree stays balanced, so `max_depth=6` means 64 leaves, each reached by exactly 6 conditions.

LightGBM grows **leaf-wise** (best-first): each iteration splits whichever *existing leaf* promises the largest loss reduction, and stops at `num_leaves`. The tree is deliberately unbalanced — depth is an emergent property, not a setting (`max_depth=-1` by default).

### The porting bug

Migrating from XGBoost, people translate `max_depth=6` into `num_leaves=64`. But a leaf-wise tree with 64 leaves is far more expressive than a balanced depth-6 tree. In the degenerate case it's a chain: 64 leaves at depth 63, where the deepest leaf conditions on 63 splits. Same leaf count, radically more capacity.

Rule of thumb: `num_leaves` should be **well under** `2**max_depth`. For an effective depth of 6, start near 20–30, not 64.

### The real guard is rows per leaf

`num_leaves` is a capacity budget; `min_data_in_leaf` (default 20) is what stops a leaf from memorizing. Average rows per leaf:

```
avg_rows_per_leaf ≈ n_train / num_leaves
```

- n=500,000, num_leaves=255 → ~1,960 rows/leaf. Healthy.
- n=50,000, num_leaves=255 → ~196. Fine.
- n=5,000, num_leaves=255 → ~20. You are *at* the floor. Growth halts early, so you silently get fewer leaves than requested while the leaves you do get sit on noise.

That last case is the common one, and the symptom is a training loss that keeps falling while validation flattens by iteration 40.

```python
params = dict(
    objective="binary",
    num_leaves=31,          # capacity budget
    max_depth=8,            # belt-and-braces cap; -1 means unlimited
    min_data_in_leaf=100,   # scale with n, not left at 20
    learning_rate=0.05,
    feature_fraction=0.8,
    bagging_fraction=0.8, bagging_freq=1,
)
```

Check what you actually grew rather than what you asked for:

```python
df = model.booster_.trees_to_dataframe()
print(df.groupby("tree_index")["node_depth"].max().describe())
print(df.loc[df.left_child.isna(), "count"].quantile([.01, .5]))
```

If observed max depth is 25 on tabular data, or the 1st-percentile leaf holds 20 rows, lower `num_leaves` and raise `min_data_in_leaf` before you touch anything else.

## 15:02 - [Crypto & Business] Flash Loans: Borrowing Millions With No Collateral, Because Repayment Is Enforced by the Transaction Itself

Every normal loan needs collateral because the lender can't force you to repay. On-chain, atomicity removes that problem: a transaction either completes entirely or reverts as if it never happened. So a lender can hand you $10M, call your code, and check the balance afterward — if you're short, the whole thing unwinds, including the transfer out.

**The mechanics.** You don't "take out" a flash loan; the pool calls you back.

1. You call `flashLoan(asset, amount)` on the pool.
2. The pool transfers the tokens and invokes your contract's callback.
3. Inside the callback you do whatever you want with the money.
4. On return, the pool asserts `balance >= before + fee`. If not, `revert`.

Aave V3 charges 5 bps (0.05%); Uniswap V2/V3 "flash swaps" effectively charge the pool's swap fee, 30 bps on a standard V3 pair. Everything must fit in one transaction's gas budget.

**Worked arbitrage.** ETH is $2,500 on pool A and $2,512 on pool B — a 0.48% gap.

- Borrow 4,000 ETH-worth, $10,000,000, from Aave.
- Buy on A, sell on B: gross spread $48,000.
- Minus Aave fee $5,000, minus ~0.30% round-trip pool fees on $10M ≈ $30,000, minus ~$80 gas.
- Net ≈ **$12,900** — on zero capital.

Note how thin that is: the same trade at a 0.2% gap loses money. Flash loan profitability is dominated by fees, not by the headline spread.

**Why they appear in exploit post-mortems.** Flash loans are rarely the vulnerability; they're the funding. An attacker who needs $200M to push a thin pool's spot price far enough to fool a lending protocol's oracle can rent it for 5 bps instead of owning it. The bug is the oracle (or a governance vote counting current token balances). Defenses are the same with or without flash loans: TWAP or multi-source oracles, and snapshot-based voting that ignores tokens acquired this block.
