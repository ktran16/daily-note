## 00:02 - [Data Engineering] Spark Data Skew: Diagnosing and Fixing the Straggler Task

A Spark job where 199 of 200 tasks finish in seconds and one runs for 40 minutes is not slow — it is skewed. One partition holds far more rows than the rest, and since a stage completes only when its slowest task does, that single straggler sets your wall clock and your cluster bill.

**Diagnosing.** In the Spark UI, open the stage and look at the task duration and shuffle-read-size distribution. If the max is orders of magnitude above the 75th percentile, you have skew. Confirm the culprit key:

```sql
SELECT customer_id, COUNT(*) AS n
FROM events
GROUP BY customer_id
ORDER BY n DESC
LIMIT 20;
```

Usually you'll find a sentinel: `NULL`, `-1`, `'unknown'`, or a genuine whale account absorbing 30% of the rows.

**Fix 1 — Broadcast the small side.** If the dimension fits in executor memory (roughly under a few hundred MB), broadcasting eliminates the shuffle entirely, so skew stops mattering:

```python
from pyspark.sql.functions import broadcast
fact.join(broadcast(dim_customer), "customer_id")
```

**Fix 2 — Let AQE handle it.** Adaptive Query Execution splits oversized shuffle partitions at runtime. On Spark 3.x it's on by default, but verify the thresholds actually trigger:

```python
spark.conf.set("spark.sql.adaptive.enabled", True)
spark.conf.set("spark.sql.adaptive.skewJoin.enabled", True)
spark.conf.set("spark.sql.adaptive.skewJoin.skewedPartitionFactor", 5)
spark.conf.set("spark.sql.adaptive.skewJoin.skewedPartitionThresholdInBytes", "256MB")
```

AQE only rescues sort-merge joins on shuffled data — it will not help a skewed `groupBy` on a single key.

**Fix 3 — Salting.** When the hot key is real and both sides are large, spread it across N buckets and explode the small side to match:

```python
from pyspark.sql import functions as F
N = 50
left  = fact.withColumn("salt", (F.rand() * N).cast("int"))
right = (dim.withColumn("salt", F.explode(F.array([F.lit(i) for i in range(N)]))))
left.join(right, ["customer_id", "salt"])
```

You pay an N-fold increase in the small side's size — keep N just large enough to flatten the histogram.

**Fix 4 — Filter first.** Nulls that will never match belong outside the join. `WHERE customer_id IS NOT NULL` before the shuffle is often the entire fix, and it costs nothing.

Always re-check the task histogram after the change: skew fixes that "look right" but move the imbalance elsewhere are common.

## 05:03 - [Data Analytics] Funnel Analysis in SQL: Ordered Steps, Time Windows, and Honest Denominators

Most "funnel" queries are wrong in the same way: they count `COUNT(DISTINCT user_id)` per event type and divide. That silently credits a user who viewed checkout on Monday and had signed up in March, and it counts steps that happened *out of order* as conversions.

A correct funnel needs three things: a **per-user anchor**, an **ordering constraint**, and a **time window**.

**The pattern.** Anchor each user at their first step-1 event, then look for the earliest later step that follows it within the window:

```sql
WITH anchored AS (
  SELECT user_id, MIN(event_at) AS t0
  FROM events
  WHERE event_name = 'view_product'
    AND event_at >= DATE '2026-08-01'
  GROUP BY user_id
),
steps AS (
  SELECT
    a.user_id,
    MIN(e.event_at) FILTER (WHERE e.event_name = 'add_to_cart')     AS t1,
    MIN(e.event_at) FILTER (WHERE e.event_name = 'begin_checkout')  AS t2,
    MIN(e.event_at) FILTER (WHERE e.event_name = 'purchase')        AS t3
  FROM anchored a
  JOIN events e
    ON e.user_id = a.user_id
   AND e.event_at >  a.t0
   AND e.event_at <= a.t0 + INTERVAL '7 days'
  GROUP BY a.user_id
)
SELECT
  COUNT(*)                                             AS s0_viewed,
  COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE t1 IS NOT NULL)               AS s1_carted,
  COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE t1 < t2)                      AS s2_checkout,
  COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE t1 < t2 AND t2 < t3)          AS s3_purchased
FROM steps;
```

The `t1 < t2 < t3` chain is what enforces order. Drop it and users who hit `purchase` via a saved link — never touching cart — inflate your checkout conversion.

**Read the numbers two ways.** Given 100,000 / 24,000 / 18,500 / 12,900:

- *Step-to-step*: 24.0% → 77.1% → 69.7%. Tells you **where** to fix.
- *Overall from top*: 24.0% → 18.5% → 12.9%. Tells you **what it's worth**.

Reporting only the second hides that add-to-cart, at 24%, is the real leak.

**Two traps.** First, the window truncates: on 2026-08-23 a user anchored on 08-20 still has 4 days left, so recent cohorts look worse than they are — cut the anchor range at `today - 7 days`. Second, strict funnels punish legitimate backtracking; if users routinely re-enter earlier steps, measure *any* ordered occurrence rather than the first, or your product looks broken when it isn't.

## 10:03 - [ML & AI] Data Leakage: Why Your 0.99 AUC Is a Lie

Leakage is when information that won't exist at prediction time sneaks into training. It's the single most common reason a model that scores brilliantly offline collapses in production — and it never shows up as an error, only as a suspiciously good number.

**The three flavors.**

*Target leakage* — a feature is a downstream consequence of the label. Predicting hospital readmission with a `discharge_medication_changed` flag that only gets set after the readmission decision. Rule of thumb: for every feature, ask "at the moment I score a row, would this value already be populated?" If it's populated by a process the label triggers, drop it.

*Preprocessing leakage* — fitting a transform on all the data before splitting:

```python
# WRONG — scaler saw the test set's mean/std
X = StandardScaler().fit_transform(X)
X_tr, X_te = train_test_split(X, test_size=0.2)

# RIGHT — fit inside the fold
pipe = Pipeline([("sc", StandardScaler()), ("clf", LogisticRegression())])
cross_val_score(pipe, X, y, cv=5)   # scaler refits per fold
```

Same applies to imputation, target/mean encoding, SMOTE, and feature selection. Put every fitted step inside a `Pipeline` and leakage becomes structurally impossible.

*Temporal / group leakage* — random splits on time-ordered or repeated-entity data. If one customer has 40 rows split across train and test, you're testing memorization. Use `GroupKFold(groups=customer_id)` or, for forecasting, `TimeSeriesSplit`, which only ever trains on the past.

**Detection.** Three cheap smells worth running before you celebrate:

1. Any single feature with AUC > 0.95 on its own. Check it.
2. A gap between CV score and a true holdout from a *later* time period.
3. Permutation importance dominated by one column — inspect how it's produced upstream.

**Worked case.** A churn model hit 0.97 AUC. Feature: `days_since_last_login`. For churned users the pipeline computed it as of extract date, so churners had huge values by construction. Recomputed as of the prediction cutoff, honest AUC was 0.71. That 0.71 model made money; the 0.97 one would have shipped garbage scores on day one.

The discipline: define a prediction timestamp, and build every feature strictly from data available before it.

## 15:02 - [Crypto & Business] Freelance Rate Math: Why Your Hourly Rate Is Not Your Salary ÷ 2080

The most common freelancing mistake is anchoring to your old salary. A $150k employee divides by 2,080 hours, gets ~$72/hr, rounds to $80, and quietly takes a large pay cut.

**What the salary hides.** Employment bundles costs your client no longer pays: employer payroll taxes (~7.65% in the US, which you now owe as the self-employment half), health insurance ($600–1,200/mo for a family plan on the individual market), retirement match, paid time off, equipment, software, and unpaid time between engagements.

**The real denominator: billable hours.** Nobody bills 40 hours a week. Sales calls, proposals, invoicing, bookkeeping, and marketing consume 30–40% of your week. Then add gaps between clients. A realistic solo consultant bills **1,000–1,300 hours a year**, not 2,080.

**Worked example — target $150k take-home equivalent:**

```
Target personal income              $150,000
+ Self-employment tax delta (~7.65%)  11,500
+ Health insurance ($900/mo)          10,800
+ Retirement (10% self-funded)        15,000
+ Software, hardware, accounting       6,000
+ 3 weeks unpaid vacation/sick      (in hours below)
= Required revenue                  $193,300

Billable hours: 25/wk x 46 wks     = 1,150
Rate = 193,300 / 1,150             ≈ $168/hr
```

So $168/hr — more than double the naive $72 — is roughly *break-even parity* with the old job, not a premium. Most people should quote $185–200 to leave margin for scope creep and slow payers.

**Three practical moves:**

1. **Quote projects, not hours.** A fixed $18k scope hides your rate and rewards you for being fast. Hourly billing penalizes efficiency.
2. **Anchor to client value, not your cost.** A pipeline that saves a client $400k/yr justifies $40k regardless of how many hours it took.
3. **Raise on new clients first.** Test a 20% higher number on the next prospect; keep existing clients at the old rate until renewal.

Track your *effective* rate — total revenue ÷ total hours worked, including unbilled ones. That number is the honest one.

*Educational content, not financial advice. Tax and insurance figures are illustrative US examples; consult a professional for your situation.*

## 19:03 - [PC Hardware] GPU VRAM: Why Gaming and ML Need Different Amounts (and Why Bandwidth Decides Speed)

VRAM is the spec most people get wrong, because gaming and ML fail in completely different ways when you run short.

**Gaming degrades gracefully.** Exceed VRAM and the driver spills textures to system RAM over PCIe. You get frame-time stutter and pop-in, not a crash — and dropping textures from Ultra to High usually costs 300–800 MB with almost no visual difference. Rough 2026 floors: 8 GB is fine at 1080p but hits walls with ray tracing at 1440p; 12 GB is the practical 1440p minimum; 16 GB is comfortable for 4K. Frame generation adds roughly 1–1.5 GB on top.

**ML fails hard.** One byte over and you get `CUDA out of memory`. The model must fit, period. Estimate it:

```
weights (GB) ≈ params (B) × bytes_per_param
  FP16 = 2.0   INT8 = 1.0   Q4_K_M ≈ 0.55
```

| Model | FP16 | Q8 | Q4 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 8B | 16 GB | 8 GB | 4.5 GB |
| 32B | 64 GB | 32 GB | 18 GB |
| 70B | 140 GB | 70 GB | 40 GB |

Then add KV cache — often the thing that actually kills you at long context:

```
KV (GB) ≈ 2 × layers × kv_heads × head_dim × ctx × bytes ÷ 1e9
```

For an 8B model at 32k context that's ~2 GB in FP16, ~1 GB with KV quantized to int8. Budget ~1 GB for CUDA context and activations too.

**Bandwidth sets token speed, not core count.** Autoregressive generation reads the entire model from VRAM once per token, so:

```
tokens/sec ≈ memory_bandwidth ÷ model_size_in_bytes
```

A 5 GB quantized model on a 960 GB/s card tops out near 190 tok/s theoretical; expect 60–75% of that in practice. This is why a card with more but slower VRAM can *load* a bigger model and still feel sluggish, and why unified-memory systems with 200–500 GB/s trail discrete GPUs despite huge capacity.

**Practical rule:** buy capacity for the largest model you actually intend to run, then bandwidth for how fast it runs. Prompt processing is compute-bound and scales with cores; generation is bandwidth-bound and doesn't.
